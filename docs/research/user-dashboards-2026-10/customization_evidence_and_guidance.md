# Customisable dashboards: evidence on uptake, wishes, guidance and criticism (state: 13 October 2026)

Reading aid for every finding below:

- **[V]** = verified: I opened the primary source in this session and the quote or number is from its text.
- **[S]** = snippet only: the claim comes from a search-result summary of the named page; I could not open the page itself (403/404/blocked) or did not. Treat wording and numbers as unconfirmed.
- Vote counts on idea portals are as displayed on 13 October 2026.
- Context labels: *consumer* (general public, occasional use), *office* (general knowledge workers), *expert* (frequent professional users of one tool).

## 1. How many users customise at all? Default bias and customisation rates

### Takeaway
Across consumer and general-office software the measured share of people who rearrange an interface is small (majority-on-default at My Yahoo!, under 2% of Office 2003 sessions with customised toolbars), but every one of those sources also reports a heavy-use minority that customises a lot, and two controlled studies show that rates jump to a clear majority when the mechanism is light and the user is a frequent one (92% of 101 WordPerfect users; 14 of 19 in a four-week Word field study). The famous "fewer than 5%" figure is a blog anecdote without a published method; several other percentages in circulation are folklore.

### Cited Findings

**The "5%" figure and its status**
- [V] Jared Spool, blog post dated 14 September 2011, about Microsoft Word settings files: "We asked a ton of people to send us their settings file"; "Several hundred folks did just that"; "Less than 5% of the users we surveyed had changed any settings at all." No exact sample size, no year of the study, no publication beyond the blog. Context: *office*, general Word users. — [UIE Brain Sparks](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/)
- [V] Same post, the exception: programmers and designers "often had changed more than 40% (and some had changed as much as 80%) of the options in the program"; "If you're a programmer or designer, then you're not like most people." — [UIE Brain Sparks](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/)
- [V] Same post, why defaults are trusted: autosave was off by default because "a programmer had made a decision to initialize the config.ini file with all zeroes"; interviewed users assumed the default was deliberate. — [UIE Brain Sparks](https://archive.uie.com/brainsparks/2011/09/14/do-users-change-their-settings/)
- [S] A repost of a Guardian piece attributes the same study to "Microsoft researchers" asking for configuration files. That conflicts with Spool's own account (his team, UIE). Treat the Microsoft attribution as an error of retelling. — [Pixel Envy linklog](https://pxlnv.com/linklog/why-default-settings-should-be-right/)
- [S] Figures such as "over 80% of users never change software defaults" and "approximately 60% of users interpret the default option as recommended" circulate attributed to Nielsen Norman Group; the search summary itself could not trace them to any NN/g primary source. **Folklore until located.** — [NN/g video page that the secondary articles point to](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/the-danger-of-defaults/)

**Product telemetry (the strongest numbers)**
- [V] Microsoft Office 2003, Customer Experience Improvement Program data, published by Jensen Harris (Office UI lead) on 27 June 2006: "Looking across a hundred million or so people using Office 2003 ... In fewer than 2% of sessions, the program was running with customized command bars. Of the 2% of sessions with customizations present, 85% included customization of four or fewer commands." Precisely "~1.9% of sessions"; moving a whole toolbar and add-in buttons were not counted. Unit is **sessions, not users**. Context: *office*. — [Jensen Harris, "Let's Talk About Customization"](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/jensenh/lets-talk-about-customization)
- [V] Same post: "The most popular single customization? Removing the 'Read' button from the Standard toolbar in Word." And: "Finding so many patterns in the customizations was a heartening revelation, because it implied that if we got enough of the details of the command organization right based on these common customizations, many fewer customizations might be necessary." — [Jensen Harris](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/jensenh/lets-talk-about-customization)
- [V] Same post, the design consequence: "we took a pragmatic approach and decided to focus on the 99.7% case: people who don't take advantage of customization or only use it to customize four or fewer commands. Out of this goal was born the Quick Access Toolbar", with the goal of "zero customization complexity". And the cost of that choice: "0.3% of the 450 million paid Office customers still represents 1.35 million people", with a "strong correlation between expert users and customization". — [Jensen Harris](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/jensenh/lets-talk-about-customization)
- [V] Limits of that data, raised in the comments of the same post: enterprises often disabled telemetry by policy; "sessions" undercounts heavy users who produce most of the work per session; one commenter does not customise because settings do not travel across machines and versions; another asks why a team cannot share one customised toolbar and why customisation has no undo. The widely repeated "98% of people never customised their toolbars" is a misreading of the session figure. — [Jensen Harris, comments](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/jensenh/lets-talk-about-customization)
- [V] My Yahoo! (customisable portal page of modules, the closest historical analogue to a widget dashboard), Manber, Patel, Robison, *Communications of the ACM* 43(8), August 2000: "A very surprising statistic is the majority of active My Yahoo! users do not customize their pages. They work with the default page." Offered reasons: "1. Our default page is so good there is no reason to put more effort. 2. Our customization tools are so difficult to use that many people do not bother. 3. Many people do not need complex personalization." Conclusion: "A great deal of effort should go into the default page." Context: *consumer*. No percentage given. — [Manber et al. 2000 (PDF copy)](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/cps049s/spring07/Notes/p35-manber.pdf)
- [V] Same paper, the counterweight: "Power users will do amazing things—never underestimate them ... We have My Yahoo! customized pages bigger than 500KB, with stock portfolios of more than 200 stocks ... If we had designed the system never to allow it, we would have missed out"; "Do not use artificial limits unless they are absolutely necessary"; "Personalized features are currently of greatest benefit to power users"; challenge: "to lower the entry bar ... and making it clear to novices that customization is possible." — [Manber et al. 2000](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/cps049s/spring07/Notes/p35-manber.pdf)
- [S] BBC homepage, redesign of September 2011 dropped the draggable modules: the BBC's own post is summarised as saying the homepage "has historically enabled a measure of customization but the majority of users haven't taken advantage of this" and that users "place a higher value on being able to easily filter content, than re-order what's already there." No percentage. Context: *consumer*. I could not open the BBC page (fetch blocked). — [BBC Internet Blog, Sept 2011](http://www.bbc.co.uk/blogs/bbcinternet/2011/09/bbc_online_homepage_beta_producer.html); reaction in [The Register forum, 21 Sept 2011](https://forums.theregister.com/forum/all/2011/09/21/bbc_home_page_strategy_boutique/)

**HCI studies**
- [S] Wendy Mackay, "Triggers and barriers to customizing software", CHI 1991, pp. 153-160: customisation activity of 51 staff of MIT Project Athena (Unix) over four months; abstract: the study "suggests that most people do not carefully optimize each customization option". Reported triggers: noticing one's own repeated patterns, stopping something annoying or slow, retrofitting a new version to behave like the old one; reported barriers: lack of time first, then difficulty of the customisation mechanism. Context: *expert* technical staff, 1990. — [DBLP record](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/conf/chi/Mackay91.html), [PDF link (currently broken)](https://www.lri.fr/~mackay/pdffiles/CHI91.Triggers.pdf), [thesis abstract](https://www.lri.fr/~mackay/pdffiles/Thesis_abstract.pdf)
- [V] How later work reads Mackay: Page et al. quote her that the data "support the idea that users 'satisfice' rather than optimize"; Banovic et al. (2012) summarise "Mackay showed that most users do not customize". — [Page et al. 1996](https://chi1996.acm.org/proceedings/papers/Page/srp_txt.html), [Banovic et al. 2012](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/triggering-triggers-and-burying.pdf_recQmDHm7lgNUZAhp.pdf)
- [V] **Counter-evidence:** Page, Johnsgard, Albert, Allen, "User Customization of a Word Processor", CHI 1996: 101 participants across the United States, WordPerfect 6.0a for Windows. "A surprising 92% of the participants in this study did some form of customization of the software." "Eighty-six percent made changes to their general preferences settings"; "Seventy-seven percent of the users did something to customize their Button Bar"; visual appearance "fewer than 15%". "Participants who used the software most heavily also did the most customization (p < .05)" — the only significant variable. Recommendation: "Expect users to customize the software." Context: *office*, working users of their main tool; "customisation" is counted broadly (any preference). — [Page et al. 1996](https://chi1996.acm.org/proceedings/papers/Page/srp_txt.html)
- [V] McGrenere, Baecker, Booth, "An Evaluation of a Multiple Interface Design Solution for Bloated Software", CHI 2002: field study, 20 participants, a user-built "Personal Interface" for Word 2000 with one-click toggle to the full interface, four weeks. "14 out of 19 participants spent 50% or more of their word processing time in their Personal Interface ... and these same participants added all frequently-used functions"; "13 participants preferred Personal to either form of 2000" (the adaptive menus and the all-in-one interface). Seven of 20 had not noticed that Word 2000's menus were adapting. Context: *office*, own work, light add/remove mechanism, full interface one click away. — [McGrenere et al. 2002](https://www.dgp.toronto.edu/public_user/RMB/papers/p28.pdf)
- [S] Findlater & McGrenere, CHI 2004, static vs adaptable vs adaptive split menus, 27 participants: static faster than adaptive; adaptable not significantly slower than static once users had seen another condition first; "the majority of users preferred the adaptable menu overall". Lab study, menus not layouts. — [Findlater & McGrenere 2004](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/edapt/papers/findlater2004.pdf), summarised in [Gajos et al. 2006](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/kgajos-avi06.pdf)
- [V] Banovic, Chevalier, Grossman, Fitzmaurice, "Triggering Triggers and Burying Barriers to Customizing Software", CHI 2012 (Autodesk Research): lab experiment manipulating Mackay's factors. "increasing exposure and awareness of customization features, and adding social influence can significantly affect the user's customization behavior." "In the control group, only 25% non-power users customized, and in the awareness group, only 57%. On the other hand, in the exposure and social conditions, more non-power users customized (80% and 78% respectively)." During piloting "advanced users chose to customize, while less advanced users did not." (The extracted PDF text was partly garbled; I am confident of these sentences, not of the total N.) — [Banovic et al. 2012](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/triggering-triggers-and-burying.pdf_recQmDHm7lgNUZAhp.pdf)

**Nielsen Norman Group**
- [V] Jakob Nielsen, "The Power of Defaults", 25 September 2005: search study where "42% of users clicked the top search hit, and 8% of users clicked the second hit"; after swapping the two, "users still clicked on the top entry 34% of the time and on the second hit 12% of the time." Generalisation: "Users rely on defaults in many other areas of user interface design"; users "rarely utilize fancy customization features"; "people tend to stick to the defaults." The data is about search-result order, the customisation statement is an assertion without a number. — [NN/g 2005](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/the-power-of-defaults/)
- [V] Amy Schade, "Customization vs. Personalization in the User Experience", 10 July 2016: "Customization is done by the user", "Personalization is done by the system being used". Downsides of customisation: "many users don't know what they actually need" and "most users are not interested in doing the work required to tweak the user interface to match their preferences." No percentage. Recommendations: "Personalization and customization should enhance an already good experience, rather than try to fix a poor one"; "it's the designer's job to prioritize the ideas and create a tight base-level design". — [NN/g 2016](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/customization-personalization/)
- [V] Jakob Nielsen, "Intranet Portals: A Tool Metaphor for Corporate Information", 30 March 2003 (employees, i.e. *professional* context): role-based personalisation "uses existing databases to give users information relevant to their job"; "It's an open question whether additional personalization is sufficiently useful to be worth the effort"; most portal teams interviewed had decided against individual personalisation, and portals that offered it "found that most users didn't bother to use it." No figure. — [NN/g 2003](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/intranet-portals-a-tool-metaphor/)
- [S] NN/g video summary: "Assume that users will not change defaults and plan for this in your designs." — [NN/g, The Danger of Defaults](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/the-danger-of-defaults/)

### Inferences
- The low figures (Spool, Office, My Yahoo!, BBC, intranet portals) all come from broad populations where most people use the product incidentally and where the thing to customise was either deep settings or a general-purpose page. None of them measures frequent professional users of a monitoring or planning screen. Carrying "5%" over to operations staff is not supported by any source found here; neither is assuming a majority.
- The two findings are compatible once split by frequency of use: Page et al. found frequency of use the only significant predictor; Harris reports a "strong correlation between expert users and customization"; Manber calls power users "the opposite of users who do not customize". The honest summary is a bimodal population, with the size of the customising group set by how often the screen is used and how cheap the mechanism is.
- Mechanism cost moves the rate by a factor, not by a few points: McGrenere's add/remove-with-toggle got 14 of 19 into a personal interface; Banovic moved non-power users from 25% to about 80% just by exposing the facility. A library can influence uptake through discoverability and effort, which argues for editing in place over a separate settings screen.
- What people customise is concentrated: 85% of customised Office sessions touched four or fewer commands, and the same handful of changes recurred. For a dashboard that predicts "hide two widgets, move one up" as the typical act, not a free composition. It also means observed customisations are a signal for fixing the default.
- Portability and undo are real barriers named by users themselves (Office comments): an arrangement that does not follow the user or cannot be reverted is rationally not worth making.

### Gaps
- **No SaaS vendor has published a dashboard-customisation uptake figure that I could find.** Searches for product-team data from Atlassian, Salesforce, HubSpot, Shopify, Stripe, Linear, Notion, Datadog, Grafana and Microsoft (Dynamics, Power BI) returned documentation and feature descriptions only. The only vendor numbers are Office 2003 (toolbars, 2006) and Grafana's 60% unused-dashboards estimate (section 6), neither of which is a "share of users who rearranged their home dashboard".
- Mackay 1991 full text not read; counts and percentages for individual triggers and barriers are therefore missing.
- Spool's Word study: original year, Word version, exact N and sampling are not stated anywhere I could find. It should be cited as an anecdote from a practitioner, not as a study.
- No controlled or telemetry study found on customisation rates of widget dashboards specifically in operations, monitoring or finance.
- BBC post text not directly verified; no percentage exists in any source found.

## 2. When does customisation pay off?

### Takeaway
The evidence supports customisation for frequent, expert users whose needs diverge by role or by personal working set, on top of a strong default, and it supports role-based defaults as the first step before individual arrangement. Dashboard research asks for more end-user reconfiguration in BI, health management and personal applications, while also documenting that authors deliberately remove customisability for casual users and for organisation-wide "one picture" dashboards.

### Cited Findings
- [V] Heavy use predicts customisation: "The more the software was used, the higher the level of customization"; authors conclude "users with high levels of product use adapt the software to complete tasks more easily", against the reading that users are "too busy" to customise. — [Page et al. 1996](https://chi1996.acm.org/proceedings/papers/Page/srp_txt.html)
- [V] Expert professionals converge on the same customisations: "The PowerPoint team helped by collecting hundreds of screenshots of heavily-customized versions of PowerPoint from professional slide designers. It turns out that many of the same customizations are widespread among these users". — [Jensen Harris 2006](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/jensenh/lets-talk-about-customization)
- [V] Sarikaya, Correll, Bartram, Tory, Fisher, "What Do We Talk About When We Talk About Dashboards?" (IEEE InfoVis 2018 / TVCG January 2019), survey of 83 dashboards in 7 clusters plus a literature review: "many dashboarding tools offer little opportunity for end users to reconfigure and customize views, a feature that was reported important in BI [29,36,65], health management [15,63], learning analytics [51,58], and personal applications [21,50]." — [Sarikaya et al. 2018](https://tableau.com/sites/default/files/2023-01/DashboardsConspiracy_final.pdf)
- [V] Same paper, what "construction and composition" means in the wild: "Many dashboards allow consumers to modify the construction and composition of views. These dashboards provide flexibility for the viewer to customize the placement of views, modify the visual representations inside those views, or select the particular dimensions and measures to visualize." — [Sarikaya et al. 2018](https://tableau.com/sites/default/files/2023-01/DashboardsConspiracy_final.pdf)
- [V] Same paper, where it does **not** pay off: "An author of a static dashboard for an organization (cluster 4) may wish to eschew customization, to ensure that the entire organization is making decisions based on a complete and universal picture of the data. However, the designer of a dashboard for personal informatics (cluster 2) would likely wish to afford customization by a user who would want to include the most relevant metrics for their lived experience." — [Sarikaya et al. 2018](https://tableau.com/sites/default/files/2023-01/DashboardsConspiracy_final.pdf)
- [V] Same paper, casual users: "even seemingly 'simple' interactive features such as filters were often considered too complex for casual users"; dashboard authors "frequently described how they reduced interactive functionality or customizability to reduce complexity"; quoted advice from the literature: "The key is to ... support the information needs of power users and then turn off functionality as needed to avoid overwhelming casual users". Closing open question of the paper: "How can we support customizability and personalization for casual users?" — [Sarikaya et al. 2018](https://tableau.com/sites/default/files/2023-01/DashboardsConspiracy_final.pdf)
- [S] Tory, Bartram, Fiore-Gartland, Crisan, "Finding Their Data Voice: Practices and Challenges of Dashboard Users", IEEE CG&A 2021 (interviews with workers for whom dashboards are the main way into data): their needs are "far more diverse" than consumption-focused dashboard design implies; engagement was "often intermittent, dependent on experts, and involved an awkward assembly of tools". Abstract level only. — [Tableau Research](https://www.tableau.com/research/publications/finding-their-data-voice-practices-and-challenges-dashboard-users)
- [V] Role-based defaults first: NN/g 2003 recommends role-based personalisation from existing data (job, department, location) and found individual customisation beyond it largely unused. — [NN/g 2003](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/intranet-portals-a-tool-metaphor/)
- [V] Risk of role-based defaults: "if a user is tagged as the wrong 'type,' personalization can result in a frustrating experience"; teams "carried away with the ability to segment their user groups" found maintenance "impossible or too time consuming." (Schade, 2 October 2016) — [NN/g, 6 Tips for Successful Personalization](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/personalization/)
- [V] The default carries the product: "A great deal of effort should go into the default page ... improving the default pages for people who do not customize usually gets less attention. We believe this will always be crucial". — [Manber et al. 2000](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/cps049s/spring07/Notes/p35-manber.pdf)
- [S] Cost of an empty start, vendor example: SAP Fiori launchpad "My Home": "If 'My Home' is activated, the page is initially empty. The user can either import personalized app groups from other spaces, or add apps to the 'My Home' page manually", and showing the My Home menu at all is a user setting. SAP thus pairs an empty personal page with admin-built role pages and an import path. — [SAP Fiori design guidelines v1.139, Launchpad "My Home"](https://www.sap.com/design-system/fiori-design-web/v1-139/foundations/integration-and-services/sap-fiori-launchpad/sap-fiori-launchpad-my-home)
- [S] Constrained customisation as a shipped pattern, Shopify admin Home: four metrics shown by default (sessions, total sales, total orders, conversion rate, last 30 days); the merchant can "customize which 4 metrics display"; "Home metrics are unique to each user". A fixed frame with a choice of content, no free layout. Help page undated. — [Shopify Help Center, Shopify Home](https://help.shopify.com/en/manual/shopify-admin/shopify-home)
- [V] Templates over copies in monitoring: Grafana's guidance prefers one templated dashboard to per-person copies ("In many cases copies are being made to simply customize the view by setting template parameters"; "Prevent sprawl by using template variables"). In that domain the supported form of personal variation is parameters, not layout. — [Grafana dashboard best practices](https://grafana.com/docs/grafana/latest/visualizations/dashboards/build-dashboards/best-practices/)

### Inferences
- "Customise by exception" is the pattern the data supports: ship a role default good enough that most never touch it, let the frequent user hide, add and reorder, and treat recurring personal changes as defects of the default (Harris's reading of the Office data).
- Customisation pays off where two conditions hold together: the screen is used daily (Page), and what matters differs per person rather than per role (Sarikaya's personal cluster; a planner's own projects, a controller's own cost centres). Where the screen is an organisational statement of truth (board KPI page, wallboard), the same paper argues against it.
- For monitoring specifically, the Grafana material suggests that much apparent demand for personal dashboards is demand for personal *parameters* (which cluster, which team, which period). Options per widget and filters across widgets may satisfy it without any layout freedom.
- An empty personal dashboard is a known cost that SAP mitigates with import and role pages. A library should make the starting arrangement a first-class input (a default arrangement per role supplied by the application), not an afterthought.

### Gaps
- No quantitative evidence found that expert users in operations, monitoring or finance customise dashboards at a given rate, or that doing so improves task performance. The case rests on analogies (word processors, portals) and on the qualitative dashboard literature.
- The phrase "customise by exception" was not found as a term of art in a primary source; it is my label for the pattern.
- No published before/after data on role-based default dashboards versus one default for all.
- No source found that measures abandonment of empty dashboards.

## 3. What do users (and administrators) wish for in customisable dashboards?

### Takeaway
The best-documented wish is more layout freedom than fixed equal columns (Jira, about 1,700 votes across two tickets since 2010, never built), followed by choosing or setting the default view, per-user adjustments on top of admin defaults, and protection of shared dashboards against accidental edits. Coverage is uneven: I could verify Jira, Zendesk, Port, Square and GitLab; Salesforce IdeaExchange, Power BI/Fabric Ideas, ServiceNow, Odoo and Dynamics portals are not indexed well enough to quote titles and votes.

### Inferences
- The wishes cluster in four groups, in descending strength of evidence: (1) mixed widths, i.e. full-width rows beside narrower cards, rather than arbitrary free placement; (2) control over what the start screen is; (3) personal hide/reorder that leaves the shared default intact; (4) guardrails for shared dashboards (lock, edit permission, copy instead of edit).
- The Jira ticket asks for something modest: rows that span and columns that compare. That is a small set of widths in a column grid, not pixel freedom. Nothing found shows users asking for free-form pixel placement in a business dashboard.
- Atlassian declining a 977-vote request for sixteen years, while investing instead in "editing shared filters and dashboards", indicates that vendors rank sharing and permissions above layout richness. It also shows vote counts overstate priority: the voters are the customising minority of section 1.
- The Square thread is the mirror of the default-bias data: people who did arrange their screen treat the arrangement as property. A stored arrangement must survive product updates (new widgets, removed widgets, changed sizes) or the feature produces its angriest users.
- "Same filter 11 times" (monday) and Grafana's template variables point to dashboard-wide filters as a wish that is at least as common as layout wishes.

## 4. Design-system and vendor guidance on dashboards and customisable layouts

### Takeaway
The public design systems say a good deal about static dashboards (equal columns, three or four of them, cards in a few relative sizes, one column on phones) and almost nothing about letting the user arrange them; SAP Fiori is the one system found that specifies personalisation, edit mode, card limits and a reset to the administrator's default. For Atlassian, Polaris, Lightning, Fluent, Material, Redwood and GOV.UK I found no dashboard-arrangement guidance at all.

### Cited Findings

**PatternFly (Red Hat)**
- [V] Dashboard pattern, current site: a dashboard is cards plus a layout, "typically in a grid structure"; each card conveys "a single metric or a group of closely related metrics". Grid rules: "All columns must be the same width", "All columns must have the same sized gutter"; at standard desktop widths (1200-1400px) the columns span the full page width; three- and four-column layouts are the most common; the four-column example uses 16px gutters and 16px margins. — [PatternFly, Dashboard design guidelines](https://www.patternfly.org/patterns/dashboard/design-guidelines)
- [V] Card sizes are **three relative sizes: small, medium, large**, describing surface area rather than exact dimensions; shape follows content (lists taller, line charts wider); dynamic cards such as event lists get a maximum height and scroll; layouts should avoid gaps when a dynamic card changes height. Card types: aggregate status, trend, utilization, details, events. — [PatternFly, Dashboard design guidelines](https://www.patternfly.org/patterns/dashboard/design-guidelines)
- [V] Responsive: the only statement is that a four-column layout becomes one column on mobile, so card priority determines order. No breakpoints. **No guidance on end-user customisation, drag, edit mode, catalogue or reset** on this page. — [PatternFly, Dashboard design guidelines](https://www.patternfly.org/patterns/dashboard/design-guidelines)
- [S] PatternFly 3 (archived) said so explicitly: the dashboard layout guidance "does not currently include editable dashboard requirements." — [PatternFly 3, Dashboard Layout](https://pf3.patternfly.org/v3/pattern-library/dashboard/dashboard-layout)

**SAP Fiori**
- [S] Overview Page, fixed card layout: columns by device — "Phone: 1 column; Tablet (portrait): 2 columns; Laptop / tablet (landscape): 3 columns; Large desktop: 4 columns; Extended monitor: 5 columns (maximum)"; breakpoints set the column width to 20 or 25 rem; cards have a fixed width, "the height of the cards is flexible, and depends on the content and type of card." — [SAP Fiori v1.142, OVP fixed card layout](https://www.sap.com/design-system/fiori-design-web/v1-142/page-types/floorplans/overview-page-ovp/overview-page-fixed-card-layout/usage)
- [S] Overview Page, resizable card layout: no fixed number of columns, cards sit on an underlying grid and "do not have fixed dimensions"; users resize cards; "Whenever the size of a card changes, the content adapts automatically to show the most relevant information in the available space"; resizing "is not supported on mobile devices", but works in small desktop windows. Breakpoint table not retrieved. — [SAP Fiori v1.148, OVP resizable card layout](https://www.sap.com/design-system/fiori-design-web/v1-148/page-types/floorplans/overview-page-ovp/resizable-card-layout-overview-page/usage)
- [S] My Home in SAP S/4HANA Cloud Public Edition: fixed sections (banner, To-Dos, News, Pages, Apps, Insights Tiles, Insights Cards); Insights Cards capped at "a maximum of 10 cards"; Insights Tiles "displayed in one row"; users add, remove and reorder tiles and cards, and change card size; cards are added from overview-page apps via "Add Card to My Home" in the card's own menu (the source is the application, not a separate catalogue); a settings dialog can hide a card or section and "reset all changes to the default setting that has been defined by the key user". - [S] Launchpad spaces and pages: administrators build pages in "Manage Launchpad Pages" (role SAP_FLP_ADMIN) and assign spaces and pages to roles; "Users can easily personalize their pages directly by rearranging or adding tiles. Users have access to advanced personalization options via the edit mode"; recommended density "between 3-7 apps per section and 25 apps at most per page"; the older group-based home page is deprecated since S/4HANA 2021 FPS01. — [SAP Fiori v1.139, Launchpad Spaces](https://www.sap.com/design-system/fiori-design-web/v1-139/foundations/integration-and-services/sap-fiori-launchpad/sap-fiori-launchpad-spaces), [SAP Learning, Personalizing SAP Fiori Launchpad](https://learning.sap.com/courses/learning-the-basics-of-sap-fiori/personalizing-sap-fiori_decea017-0eda-41c1-bea9-c83627443035)

**IBM Carbon**
- [V] Data-visualisation "Dashboards" page: two kinds, presentation (KPI status) and exploration (interactive). - [S] Foreman community design meeting: "Consider having widgets Save positions automatically without having to click button." — [Foreman Community](https://community.theforeman.org/t/weekly-dev-design-meeting-dashboard/6962)

**Google Material / Android**

### Inferences
- The convergent static rules are: equal-width columns with equal gutters; three or four columns on a desktop, five at most on very wide screens (SAP), one on a phone; card width in whole columns; height either free by content (SAP fixed layout) or equal per row (Emarsys); a small named set of sizes (PatternFly's small/medium/large, Emarsys' half/full). No system found prescribes free pixel placement.
- Every system that addresses phones collapses to a single column ordered by priority. So an arrangement must carry an order as well as positions, or the narrow layout is undefined. PatternFly says it outright: priority decides the order.
- SAP is the reference for governed personalisation: administrator-defined role pages, user edit mode on top, a cap on the number of cards, a reset that returns to the key user's default rather than to a factory default, and adding a card from where it lives in the application.
- The silence of the large systems is itself a finding: user-arrangeable dashboards are treated as product-specific, and a team adopting one gets no ready guidance on edit mode, catalogue or reset. That is a gap a library's documentation can fill, and a reason not to expect users to arrive with settled conventions.
- Autosave of arrangement (Emarsys, Foreman) versus an explicit edit mode (SAP) are both in use; nothing found compares them.

### Gaps
- **PatternFly widgetized dashboards / Red Hat Hybrid Cloud Console:** no documentation found by search. From memory, not verified in this session: PatternFly publishes a "widgetized dashboard" extension that the Hybrid Cloud Console landing page uses (drag, resize, a widget drawer, reset to default). The report writer should check patternfly.org extensions directly before citing it.
- **IBM Carbon "widget" guidance:** not found on the public site; Carbon for IBM Products may hold it (not searched successfully).
- **No dashboard-arrangement guidance found** for: Atlassian Design System, Shopify Polaris (beyond the app "metrics card" composition and a homepage template), Salesforce Lightning Design System, Microsoft Fluent 2, Oracle Redwood (only Oracle Analytics docs: about eight composite visualisations per overview dashboard), GOV.UK Design System / Service Manual.
- SAP pages returned 403 on direct fetch; all SAP figures are from search summaries of versions 1.71 to 1.148 and may differ by release. The resizable layout's column-per-breakpoint table and the card size steps of My Home were not retrieved.
- Material 3 site (m3.material.io) not opened; the Android developer guide stands in for it.

## 5. Dashboard design best practice relevant to arrangement

### Takeaway
The prescriptive canon (Few) wants one screen, no scrolling, the most important information emphasised and laid out for comparison; the descriptive research (Sarikaya, Bach) shows real dashboards routinely break the one-screen rule and catalogues layouts in which position, grouping and size carry meaning. Free arrangement by users puts those meaning-carrying decisions in lay hands, so the defensible role for a library is to make the well-formed arrangement the easy one.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Stephen Few's definition: a dashboard is "a visual display of the most important information needed to achieve one or more objectives, consolidated and arranged on a single screen so it can be monitored at a glance." — quoted in [Juice Analytics](https://www.juiceanalytics.com/writing/breaking-free-one-page-dashboard-rule) [V] and [Dundas](https://dundas.com/support/blog/so-what-exactly-is-a-dashboard)
- [S] Few, "Common Pitfalls in Dashboard Design", February 2006 (ProClarity white paper; also a chapter of *Information Dashboard Design*, O'Reilly 2006): thirteen pitfalls, the first "Exceeding the Boundaries of a Single Screen", the ninth "Arranging the data poorly"; others include inadequate context, excessive detail, and "Highlighting important data ineffectively or not at all". I did not read Few's own text. — [ProClarity white paper (PDF)](https://support.itrsgroup.com/hc/en-us/article_attachments/115004392393), list in [UXmatters review, April 2007](https://uxmatters.com/mt/archives/2007/04/book-review-information-dashboard-design.php)
- [V] A secondary summary of those two pitfalls: "If certain data points need immediate attention, we need to make them stand out"; "When comparing different data points, you should arrange and visually design them specifically for comparison"; "use visual cues like bolder color, larger text size or borders to highlight key metrics". — [The Data School, part 2](https://www.thedataschool.co.uk/anh-vu/are-you-making-these-13-dashboard-design-mistakes-part-2)
- [S] Top-left priority attributed to Few: only a reader's review says "Top left for important information vs bottom right for least important" and "making sure that it's obvious where to start, and where to go". **Not verified in Few's text.** — [The Information Lab review](https://www.theinformationlab.co.uk/community/blog/information-dashboard-design-stephen)
- [S] Reading-pattern basis usually cited for top-left: Poynter Institute's Eyetrack III found readers view screen regions in an order, with particular attention to the upper left and middle left. That study concerned news homepages, not dashboards. — [Dummies, layout and placement](https://www.dummies.com/article/excel-dashboard-design-principle-use-layout-and-placement-to-draw-focus-138383)
- [V] Carbon repeats the F-pattern rule for dashboards: "Place the most important at the top of the page"; most important data gets "the highest contrast and the largest area". — [Carbon, Dashboards](https://carbondesignsystem.com/data-visualization/dashboards)
- [V] Critique of the single-screen rule (Zach Gemignani, Juice Analytics): a page limit as a rule "assumes there is only one viable means to achieve the desired ends"; other constraints could do the same work, e.g. limiting the number of key measures; the at-a-glance claim "is more self-deceiving"; cramming reduces meaning. Year not shown on the page. — [Juice Analytics](https://www.juiceanalytics.com/writing/breaking-free-one-page-dashboard-rule)
- [V] Sarikaya et al. on Few: he distinguishes "dashboards" ("single screen, used only for monitoring") from "faceted analytical displays"; their 83-dashboard survey found practice wider than that, and their framework is "descriptive rather than prescriptive". — [Sarikaya et al. 2018](https://tableau.com/sites/default/files/2023-01/DashboardsConspiracy_final.pdf)
- [V] Bach et al., "Dashboard Design Patterns" (IEEE VIS 2022 / TVCG 2023; 144 dashboards, eight groups of patterns, a workshop with 23 participants). **Screenspace patterns:** screenfit ("fully visible on screen and all information is visible at all times"), overflow (scrolling), detail-on-demand, parameterized, multiple pages; they "are not mutually exclusive and can be combined". — [Dashboard Design Patterns site](https://dashboarddesignpatterns.github.io/patterns.html), [arXiv 2205.00757](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.00757v1)
- [V] Same source, **page layout patterns:** open ("widgets of different sizes and aspect ratios are laid out in an open way without apparent specific rules"), table (aligned "into columns and rows, each with a specific semantic"), stratified ("a top-down ordering of widgets and their information"), grouped ("two or more widgets with a specific relation", shown by lines, whitespace or shading), schematic (placed by an external property "such as a physical layout"). The site's discussion of the open pattern associates tiled layouts with equal apparent importance and with user reordering (summary wording, not a verbatim quote). No frequency counts on the page. — [Dashboard Design Patterns site](https://dashboarddesignpatterns.github.io/patterns.html)
- [V] Grafana on ordering: "Try to create a logical progression of data, such as large to small or general to specific"; in a service dashboard "The row order reflects the data flow"; one row per service, rate and errors left, latency right. — [Grafana best practices](https://grafana.com/docs/grafana/latest/visualizations/dashboards/build-dashboards/best-practices/)
- [V] PatternFly on size and shape: three relative sizes; shape follows content; avoid gaps when a card's height changes. — [PatternFly, Dashboard design guidelines](https://www.patternfly.org/patterns/dashboard/design-guidelines)

### Inferences
- In Bach's vocabulary a user-arranged widget grid is the "open" layout by construction: equal-looking tiles without rules. The layouts that carry meaning (stratified, grouped, table) need structure that a bare drag-and-drop grid does not express. Sections with headings would give users "grouped"; a fixed first row for key figures would give "stratified".
- Few's single-screen rule and a user-extensible dashboard pull against each other: every added widget pushes toward overflow. The Juice critique offers the reconciling constraint, a cap on what is shown, which SAP applies (ten cards) and Shopify applies (four metrics).
- Ways a library can nudge good arrangements, each traceable to a source above: sizes from a small named set rather than free pixels (PatternFly, Emarsys); per-widget default and minimum size chosen by the widget's author so shape follows content (PatternFly); alignment to rows and no holes (Emarsys equal row height; PatternFly "avoid gaps"); new widgets placed at the end rather than the top so the default hierarchy survives (inference from stratified layout); a defined order for the one-column collapse (PatternFly); grouping by sections (Bach "grouped").
- Size as importance (Carbon) means that letting users resize is letting them restate importance. That is appropriate on a personal dashboard and questionable on a shared one, which matches Sarikaya's cluster distinction.

### Gaps
- Few's own wording on placement (top-left, centre emphasis) not verified; *Information Dashboard Design* was not accessible. The top-left attribution should be marked secondary.
- No empirical study found that tests whether user-arranged dashboards are read faster or slower than designer-arranged ones.
- No source found that applies Gestalt proximity to user-editable dashboards specifically; the grouping argument rests on Bach's pattern and Carbon's white-space advice.
- Bach's pattern frequencies (how many of the 144 use each layout) are in the paper, which I did not open beyond the abstract and the pattern site.

## 6. Criticism and failure modes

### Takeaway
The substantive criticisms are four: configurability used to avoid a design decision; the unseen engineering and test cost of every option; sprawl and abandonment (Grafana administrators estimate almost 60% of dashboards unused); and customisation flows that confuse (GitLab hid its own). I found the "couldn't decide" argument in general software-design essays, not in a dashboard-specific postmortem.

### Cited Findings

**Options as avoided decisions, and their cost**
- [V] Havoc Pennington, "Free software UI" (2002): "It turns out that preferences have a cost."; "more preferences means fewer real features, and more bugs."; "This cost of preferences is invariably underestimated by us technical types."; the de facto rule "a preference should exist if someone implements it or asks for it" is one almost no one defends; users request an option instead of reporting the problem; "Using preferences as a band-aid is the root of much UI evil."; test: ask what annoyance prompted the request, remove it for everyone if possible, and ask "is the preference in question worth 'spending' one of those slots?" — [ometer.com](https://ometer.com/preferences.html)
- [V] Jonathan Smiley (ZURB), "Customization Kills", 27 January 2009: "Customization is the lazy way out"; "figure out what's best and go with that" instead of pushing "your design problems onto the user"; customisation is "not a silver bullet, a catch-all for design problems" (he credits the phrase to Jakob Nielsen's 1998 Alertbox on personalisation, which I did not open). — [ZURB](https://zurb.com/blog/customization-kills)
- [V] NN/g: customisation and personalisation "should not be used as a fix for a broken site"; "it's the designer's job to prioritize the ideas and create a tight base-level design". — [NN/g 2016](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/customization-personalization/)
- [V] Microsoft's own trade-off statement: "we don't have unlimited design or development resources ... we had to be realistic and optimize for the most common scenarios first"; and an explicit failure mode of the old model: "it would be unacceptable for customization to cause the user interface to degrade as it did so often with Command Bars." A commenter adds the support cost: with the fixed ribbon "I am fed up of searching for their 'lost' toolbars"; another: users are "easily befuddled by 'accidental customization'"; a third values no longer having "the difficulty of explaining how to perform routine tasks to other users who do not share my customizations". — [Jensen Harris 2006](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/jensenh/lets-talk-about-customization)
- [S] A UIE seminar on dashboards frames it as a cost-benefit question: understand "what having a customizable dashboard buys you and costs you". — [UIE, Designing Dashboards](https://aycl.uie.com/virtual_seminars/designing_dashboards)

**Sprawl, abandonment, non-use**
- [V] Grafana Labs, 3 March 2021 (Agnès Toulet): "What we learned was that dashboard sprawl is a real issue"; "Administrators estimate that almost 60% of dashboards might not be used at all." An estimate by administrators, method not stated, context *expert* monitoring where anyone can create dashboards. — [Grafana blog](https://grafana.com/blog/new-in-grafana-7-4-export-usage-data-to-loki-to-help-manage-dashboard-sprawl-and-troubleshoot-faster/)
- [V] Grafana documentation: "Avoid dashboard sprawl, meaning the uncontrolled growth of dashboards."; "Dashboard sprawl negatively affects time to find the right dashboard."; "Copying dashboards with no significant changes is not a good idea" (copies miss updates to the original); maturity model: low = "no coherent dashboard management strategy. Almost everyone starts here", with "Everyone can modify your dashboards" as a symptom; high = "No editing in the browser", only approved dashboards. — [Grafana best practices](https://grafana.com/docs/grafana/latest/visualizations/dashboards/build-dashboards/best-practices/)
- [V] Datadog documentation: "A cluttered dashboard list page can make finding the right content difficult"; heuristics for abandoned dashboards: low popularity and not modified in three months; default titles ("'s dashboard", "'s screenboard") that "can indicate a test dashboard that was created quickly and never renamed"; the words "test" or "cloned". — [Datadog, Maintain relevant dashboards](https://docs.datadoghq.com/dashboards/guide/maintain-relevant-dashboards/)
- [S] Practitioner forum (projectmanagement.com): where users are meant to build their own dashboards, "few users are following the directions and creating themselves the dashboards"; another calls asking them to do so "over complicating a solution" and recommends a few well-chosen dashboard types. — [ProjectManagement.com discussion](https://www.projectmanagement.com/discussion-topic/135899/how-to-improve-the-adoption-and-usage-of-dashboards-?pageNum=1)
- [S] r/analytics, 22 March 2024 (via a mirror): an analyst built department-specific dashboards that stakeholders did not use, then was asked "can we get X data regarding my division in there?" which was already there. About consumption of dashboards, not about customising them. — [Reddit mirror](https://reddit.rtrace.io/r/analytics/comments/1bkmxm2/how_to_make_my_boss_understand_that_people_just/)
- [S] Informal survey claim: "A survey of 100 CXOs I conducted last year confirms that most of them don't want to look at dashboards, and only want insights" (fewer than 5 looked daily). Self-reported, unpublished method, executives not operators. Weak evidence. — [Substack, Art of Data Science](https://artofdatascience.substack.com/p/my-vizchitra-proposal)

**Customisation that confuses or breaks**
- [V] GitLab issue 525203 "Hide Customizable dashboard user flows": "The current Customisable dashboard experience (Creating, editing, cloning) can be quite confusing for the user"; "the feature is not fully complete or working"; "we decided to temporarily disable/hide it from the user", behind a `wip` feature flag, and to remove it from the docs. Date not shown on the page; issue number places it in early 2025. — [GitLab issue 525203](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/issues/525203)
- [S] Shared edits hit everyone: ServiceNow, "any modifications you make are applied globally"; Jira's default dashboard, same effect; HubSpot admins asking for a lock for that reason (sources in sections 3 and 4). — [ServiceNow docs](https://www.servicenow.com/docs/r/3dUp4IlSpaXzwDcoDj_v6g/5ZXgN_mtx1WoWQHkpVu5ig)
- [V] Vendor redesign destroys personal arrangements: Square, July 2025 (section 3). — [Square Community](https://community.squareup.com/t5/Online-Store/New-Dashboard-Customization/td-p/800561)
- [S] Layout does not carry across screens: Power BI phone view reverting sizes after pinning; SAP disallowing resize on mobile (sections 3 and 4). — [Microsoft Learn, Set mobile view](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/training/modules/create-dashboards-power-bi/9-set-mobile-view), [SAP Fiori v1.148, OVP resizable card layout](https://www.sap.com/design-system/fiori-design-web/v1-148/page-types/floorplans/overview-page-ovp/resizable-card-layout-overview-page/usage)
- [V] Simplicity for casual users: dashboard authors "frequently described how they reduced interactive functionality or customizability to reduce complexity". — [Sarikaya et al. 2018](https://tableau.com/sites/default/files/2023-01/DashboardsConspiracy_final.pdf)

**The other side**
- [V] Removing or withholding customisation also has a documented cost: 264 votes and 203 replies over years at Zendesk for any control at all; Manber: "If we had designed the system never to allow it, we would have missed out"; Harris: 1.35 million affected experts. — [Zendesk Community](https://community.zendesk.com/fid-0/tid-5417), [Manber et al. 2000](https://courses.cs.duke.edu/cps049s/spring07/Notes/p35-manber.pdf), [Jensen Harris 2006](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/jensenh/lets-talk-about-customization)

### Inferences
- The "we could not decide" criticism applies to a customisable dashboard exactly when it ships without a considered default. With a strong role default it does not apply; the customisation then serves real variance between people. The guide can state this as the test: could you defend the default arrangement if editing were removed?
- Sprawl is a failure of *creating* dashboards freely (Grafana, Datadog), not of *arranging* one. A library that gives each user one arrangement per surface, rather than unlimited named dashboards, avoids most of it. Multiple named dashboards per user bring the Grafana problems with them: finding, naming, cleaning up.
- Support cost when every screen differs is attested only anecdotally (Office commenters). Mitigations that follow from the sources: a visible reset, the ability for support to see or reproduce a user's arrangement, and a default that most users keep, so that most screens are in fact the same.
- GitLab's case shows that create/edit/clone as three separate flows is one flow too many for users. Editing the dashboard one is looking at, with copy as an explicit rare act, is the simpler model.
- Each widget option and each layout freedom is a "slot" in Pennington's sense: it multiplies states to test (sizes × breakpoints × widget types). Limiting sizes limits the test matrix as well as the design risk.

### Gaps
- No dashboard-specific postmortem or conference talk found that says "we made it configurable because we could not decide"; the argument is carried by general design essays (Pennington 2002, ZURB 2009).
- Hacker News and Reddit r/userexperience, r/dataengineering, r/BusinessIntelligence threads with substantive arguments on customisable dashboards were not surfaced by the search tool. The one Reddit item is a mirror snippet about dashboard non-use in general.
- No measured support-cost figure for divergent user layouts.
- No data found on how often user-arranged layouts break on another screen size; only product behaviours that sidestep it (separate phone layout, no resize on mobile).
- The Grafana 60% figure is an estimate of unknown method from 2021; no newer or measured figure found. Datadog publishes none.

## 7. Sharing and governance: personal vs team dashboards, copy, role defaults, versioning

### Takeaway
Products fall into two models: one shared dashboard that everyone with rights edits for everyone (Jira, ServiceNow, Grafana, HubSpot), where the recurring pain is accidental global edits and the answer is copy, permissions and locks; and a personal layer over an administrator's default (SAP, Dynamics, Office), where the tools are per-role defaults, disable switches and reset. Users ask for the second model inside products built on the first.

### Cited Findings
- [S] Shared-object model, ServiceNow: edits are global; edit rights follow ownership or explicit grant; duplicate yields a private copy without the sharing; access by "Restrict to roles"; only an admin changes the owner. — [ServiceNow docs](https://www.servicenow.com/docs/r/3dUp4IlSpaXzwDcoDj_v6g/5ZXgN_mtx1WoWQHkpVu5ig)
- [S] Shared-object model, Jira: the system default dashboard is edited by admins and changes reach everyone using it; users copy a dashboard to make their own. Atlassian listed "editing shared filters and dashboards" among the things it chose to build instead of flexible layout (status update of 20 March 2018 [V]). — [Atlassian Support](https://support.atlassian.com/jira-service-management-cloud/docs/manage-dashboards-and-gadgets/), [JRASERVER-21201](https://jira.atlassian.com/browse/JRASERVER-21201)
- [V] Shared-object model, Grafana: copies drift ("you miss updates to the original"); recommended instead: link to the master and vary by URL parameters and template variables; high maturity means "No editing in the browser", i.e. dashboards as reviewed, versioned artefacts. — [Grafana best practices](https://grafana.com/docs/grafana/latest/visualizations/dashboards/build-dashboards/best-practices/)
- [S] Users in the shared model asking for a personal layer: Grafana (panel positions "saved per user"; a personal dashboard per user), Port ("personalize their own dashboards without affecting others" while "Admin controls should still define the default experience" [V]), HubSpot ("Personal Saved Default Dashaboard"). — [Grafana Community](https://community.grafana.com/t/user-permissions-for-panel-layout/59658), [Port roadmap](https://roadmap.port.io/ideas/p/allow-users-to-hide-or-personalize-dashboard-widgets), [HubSpot Ideas](https://community.hubspot.com/t5/HubSpot-Ideas/Personal-Saved-Default-Dashaboard/idi-p/1667)
- [S] Admins in the shared model asking for locks and team defaults: HubSpot "Permissions to Edit Sales Dashboard", "Set Dashboard View for All Users"; Zendesk "admin to be able to edit or select a default view" [V]; Grafana "editable/non-editable only by the author". — [HubSpot Ideas](https://community.hubspot.com/t5/HubSpot-Ideas/Set-Dashboard-View-for-All-Users/idi-p/254453), [Zendesk Community](https://community.zendesk.com/fid-0/tid-5417), [Grafana Community](https://community.grafana.com/t/make-dashboards-editable-non-editable-only-by-the-author/282)
- [S] Personal-layer model, SAP: admin-built pages assigned to roles; user edit mode; My Home reset returns to "the default setting that has been defined by the key user"; a three-tier chain of vendor default, key-user default, personal change. — [SAP Fiori v1.148, My Home](https://www.sap.com/design-system/fiori-design-web/v1-148/discover/sap-products/sap-s4hana-only/s4hana-product-home-page-my-home), [SAP Fiori v1.139, Launchpad Spaces](https://www.sap.com/design-system/fiori-design-web/v1-139/foundations/integration-and-services/sap-fiori-launchpad/sap-fiori-launchpad-spaces)
- [S] Personal-layer model, Microsoft Dynamics: Business Central "Disable personalization" per profile and admin clearing of a user's personalisation; Finance and Operations admin view of who personalised what, per-user permission to personalise, and pushing one personalisation to a role or to all users (add to or replace theirs); workspace "Clear" as reset. A side effect noted for Business Central: disabling personalisation also removes bookmarks and saved views. — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/previous-versions/dynamicsnav-2018/ui-personalization-manage), [Logan Consulting](https://www.loganconsulting.com/blog/mass-personalization-in-dynamics-365-for-finance-and-operations/), [yzhums.com](https://yzhums.com/20791/)
- [S] Business Central, upgrade handling: a page is "locked" from personalisation when the user's earlier personalisation came from an older version; unlocking clears it. The product's answer to arrangements that outlive the schema they were made against. — [Why a Page is Locked from Personalization](https://bcdocs.staedean.com/md/en-US/ui-personalization-locked.md)
- [V] Sharing customisations between colleagues was asked of Office in 2006 ("Why is it so difficult/impossible for a documentation team ... share the same customized toolbar?"; "Where's the official Microsoft community site for sharing customizations") and social exposure raised customisation among non-power users to 78% in Banovic's experiment. — [Jensen Harris, comments](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/archive/blogs/jensenh/lets-talk-about-customization), [Banovic et al. 2012](https://research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/triggering-triggers-and-burying.pdf_recQmDHm7lgNUZAhp.pdf)
- [V] Organisational reason for governance: an organisation-wide dashboard may "eschew customization, to ensure that the entire organization is making decisions based on a complete and universal picture of the data." — [Sarikaya et al. 2018](https://tableau.com/sites/default/files/2023-01/DashboardsConspiracy_final.pdf)

### Inferences
- A React library does not own storage or permissions, but its data model decides which governance the host application can build. The minimum that the product evidence asks for: an arrangement is a serialisable value separate from the default; reset means "back to the default the application supplies" (SAP's key-user default); the application can render the dashboard read-only (lock) and can swap the default per role.
- "Copy to a colleague" follows for free if the arrangement is a plain value; no product evidence asks for more than that (no live-linked personal layouts).
- Versioning in the sense of history appears only in the monitoring products (Grafana's reviewed dashboards). In the business products "versioning" surfaces as the upgrade problem: an old stored arrangement meeting new widget types. Business Central locks and clears; Square overwrote. A stored arrangement needs a tolerant reader: unknown widget types dropped, new default widgets offered rather than silently missing.
- The two models should not be mixed on one surface: where a dashboard is shared and editable by many, personal overrides make "what does my colleague see?" unanswerable, which is the support cost named in section 6.

### Gaps
- Microsoft Dynamics 365 Finance and Operations statements rest on third-party blogs (2018 and later); the current Microsoft Learn pages were not opened.
- Salesforce (dynamic dashboards, "view as", folder sharing, the dynamic-dashboard limit) not verified; only a 2018 community answer mentioning "a limit of 5 dynamic dashboards in Lightning" surfaced, which may be edition-specific and out of date.
- No evidence found on how many users actually use copy/share of dashboards, or on demand for version history of personal arrangements.
- Linear, Notion, Stripe and Datadog sharing models were not researched for lack of budget; Datadog's guide says only "Avoid deleting shared dashboards."
