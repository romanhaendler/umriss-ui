# UX research and standards: paging, page size, layout stability, empty and loading states (state 2026-10-30)

Method note. Read on 2026-10-30. NN/g, Baymard, web.dev and W3C pages were read through a summarising fetch tool. A phrase in quotation marks below is one the tool returned as a quotation, or one I checked in the downloaded text. Where I could get the full text (Smith & Mosier 1986, NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, the WAI-ARIA 1.2 spec, the Layout Instability spec, web.dev's CLS page, Findlater & McGrenere 2004, Gajos et al. 2008, Heer & Robertson 2007, Bederson & Boltman 1999, Tversky et al. 2002), I downloaded it, turned it into text with `pdftotext` or a tag stripper, and quote it verbatim. "Abstract only" means I read the abstract but not the paper. "[S]" means I saw it only in a search-result summary. ISA-18.2, ISA-101 and EEMUA 191 are paywalled and were not read: ISA forbids putting its texts into AI tools, and only the table of contents of EEMUA 191 is public. Every finding is labelled with the setting it comes from, because most pagination research is about e-commerce or web search, not data tables. Anything I remember but did not re-read today is under Inferences or Gaps.

## 1. Pagination vs infinite scroll vs "load more": when each, for tables and data-dense work

### Takeaway
No source finds one pattern best overall. The evidence splits by task. Infinite scroll suits aimless browsing of homogeneous items. Pagination suits goal-directed finding, re-finding ("it's on page 3"), knowing where the end is, and keyboard and screen-reader access. "Load more" sits in between and is Baymard's e-commerce favourite. For data displays, the human-factors guidelines (Smith & Mosier 1986; NUREG-0700) accept both paging and scrolling, but they demand position cues ("page x of y"), column headers that stay visible, and controlled movement. They add one point the web literature lacks: paging is fine for finding a specific item, but not when the user must see relationships across a set of data. No study compares the patterns on data tables.

### Cited Findings
- [web, general] NN/g (Loranger, 2014): "Endless scrolling is not recommended for goal-oriented finding tasks". With pagination "It's much easier for people to remember that the item is on page 3" and "there is a beginning and an end". No study or sample size is given. — [Infinite Scrolling Is Not for Every Website](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/infinite-scrolling/)
- [web, general] NN/g (Neusesser, 2022): "There is no solution (infinite scroll, pagination, Load More, or integrated pagination) that is overall superior". Infinite scroll "works best for situations where users will want to scroll through homogeneous items". "In an infinite list of items, it is hard to remember the location of any specific item and return to it". "screen-reader users will only see the first “chunk” of the list". Load More restores access to the footer. — [Infinite Scrolling: When to Use It, When to Avoid It](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/infinite-scrolling-tips/)
- [web, general] Nielsen (2013): "pagination is usually better than infinite scrolling". "Linear content flows … should almost never be broken up into multiple screens." — [Users' Pagination Preferences and "View All"](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/item-list-view-all/)
- [e-commerce] NN/g (Moran, 2022): "Mega retailers like Amazon should stick with traditional pagination controls". Show More and infinite loading suit small or medium sets. Always show the total, how many items have loaded and how many remain. — [Alternatives to Pagination on Product-Listing Pages](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/alternatives-pagination-listing-pages/)
- [e-commerce] Baymard (Holst, 2016; studies of "more than 50+ leading e-commerce websites", number of subjects not stated): with pagination, subjects browsed far less of the list and found "more than a handful of pagination links" discouraging. Most used Next and Previous, not the numbered links. Infinite scroll led to "vastly more products" being browsed, but with less focus on each. Over 90 % of benchmarked "Load more" sites broke the Back button. For search results, never use infinite scrolling. The article cautions: "Performance might vary on other types of websites." — [Smashing Magazine, Infinite Scrolling, Pagination Or "Load More" Buttons?](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2016/03/pagination-infinite-scrolling-load-more-buttons/)
- [e-commerce] Baymard 2024 update (219 think-aloud sessions on 12 sites): pagination moved from "avoid and use “load more” instead" to "“load more” is still preferred, though pagination can work okay, but there are many details to get right". — [2024 E-Commerce Product Finding](https://baymard.com/blog/product-finding-2024-launch)
- [mobile web search, peer-reviewed, abstract only] Kim et al., CIKM 2016: on a touch phone, participants using pagination "were more likely to find relevant documents, especially those over the fold". They were also faster, and the speed difference was mostly "the time taken for the scroll itself". Sample size not seen. — [Microsoft Research listing](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=434346)
- [web, peer-reviewed, abstract only] Sharma & Murano, First Monday 2020, compared normal scrolling with pagination, infinite scrolling, infinite scrolling with Load More, and infinite scrolling with pagination: "no single scrolling method stood out as being the most usable". — [First Monday 25(3)](https://www.firstmonday.org/ojs/index.php/fm/article/view/10309)
- [reading, peer-reviewed, S] Sanchez & Wiley 2009: a scrolling format lowered comprehension of complex texts, especially for readers with lower working-memory capacity. — [Human Factors 51(5), doi](https://doi.org/10.1177/0018720809352788)
- [data displays, guideline] Smith & Mosier 1986, 2.7.2/2 "Easy Paging": "Paging is acceptable when the user is looking for a specific data item, but not when the user must discern some relationship in a set of data." 2.7.2/1: "include all data relevant to a user’s current transaction in one display frame or page … Do not rely on a user to remember data accurately from one display frame to another." — [Guidelines for Designing User Interface Software, ESD-TR-86-278 (NUS copy)](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~cs3283/ftp/guidelines.pdf)
- [data displays, guideline] Smith & Mosier 2.7.2/4: "For a large table that exceeds the capacity of one display frame, ensure that users can see column headings and row labels in all displayed sections of the table." — [same](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~cs3283/ftp/guidelines.pdf)
- [data tables] NN/g (Laubheimer, 2022) names four table tasks: "Find record(s) that fit specific criteria", "Compare data", "View, edit or add a single row's data", "Take action(s) on records". It says "if people need to compare data from nonadjacent rows, it can be difficult to move back and forth between them". The article does not discuss pagination. — [Data Tables: Four Major User Tasks](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/data-tables/)
- [nuclear control rooms, US regulator's review guideline] NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, 2.5.6-1: "Paging and windowing should not be used when searching through continuous text data." 2.5.6-14 "Discrete Paging": "When moving over multiple pages, the movement should be discrete, with no display of intermediate pages". 2.5.6-15: scrolling and paging structures "should indicate both the absolute and relative positions of the user". — [NUREG-0700 Rev. 3 (OSTI full text)](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)

### Inferences
- Comparing nonadjacent rows (NN/g) and seeing relationships across a set (Smith & Mosier) both argue for showing as many rows at once as the frame allows. That is an argument for filling, not for pagination as such.
- Re-finding by page number ("page 3") only works while the page boundaries stay put. A page size that follows height breaks the very memory that is pagination's main advantage over infinite scroll (see §3).
- Every pro-scrolling result comes from browsing tasks. Data tables in umriss's settings (alarm lists, schedules, records) are goal-directed, so the web findings lean towards paging or a bounded scroll.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study of pagination versus scrolling for data tables or grids. Every comparison found is about e-commerce lists, web search or reading.
- The full texts of Kim et al. 2016 and Sharma & Murano 2020 were not available (403 or empty), so sample sizes and task-level results are missing.

## 2. How many items per page; does page size change behaviour; do users use page-size selectors

### Takeaway
Page size does change behaviour. In the one peer-reviewed lab study of results-per-page (Kelly & Azzopardi, 36 subjects), people given 3 results per page paged until they had seen about 10, and smaller pages concentrated attention on the earlier results. Page-size selectors are, in Baymard's 2024 wording, "acceptable (if likely underused)". Nielsen advises one good default, or two well-separated choices such as 10 and 50, and remembering the user's choice. Users seldom change settings, so the default and the behaviour without configuration matter far more than the selector.

### Cited Findings
- [web search, peer-reviewed, abstract only] Kelly & Azzopardi, SIGIR 2015: "a laboratory experiment with 36 subjects … three, six or ten" results per page. Click distributions "differed significantly depending on SERP size". "those who interacted with three results per page viewed significantly more SERPs per query; interestingly, the number of SERPs they viewed per query corresponded to about 10 search results". The ten-per-page group "viewed and saved significantly more documents", and "behavior changes with SERP size, such that more time is spent focused on earlier results when SERP size decreases". — [Enlighten record](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/106761/), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1145/2766462.2767732)
- [web, general] Nielsen 2013: page-size menus are "often overkill, as when pop-up menus let users View 10, 20, 30, 40 items per screen". "It's usually better to offer a single default number", or "the choice between two numbers, say 10 and 50", with the second "substantially bigger than the default". "Please do remember users' preferences once they've gone to the trouble of stating them." A View All option should have "an upper limit … around 100 items". — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/item-list-view-all/)
- [e-commerce] Baymard 2024: "“items per page” is acceptable (if likely underused) for a pagination-loading schema". This replaces its earlier "avoid “items per page”". — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/product-finding-2024-launch)
- [e-commerce] Baymard 2016 initial loads: categories 10–30 items, then lazy-loading, with "Load more" after 50–100. Search 25–75 items. Mobile 15–30. — [Smashing Magazine](https://www.smashingmagazine.com/2016/03/pagination-infinite-scrolling-load-more-buttons/)
- [customisation, peer-reviewed, S] Mackay, CHI 1991: 51 users followed over four months; most did not customise, and lack of time was the main barrier. — [bibliographic record](https://bibtex.github.io/CHI-1991-Mackay.html), [PDF](https://www.lri.fr/~mackay/pdffiles/CHI91.Triggers.pdf) (blocked to the fetch tool today)
- [web search, S, secondary press] Google's 2006 test of 10, 20 and 30 results per page: 30 per page reportedly cut searches by about 20 %, which was attributed to slower page generation. This is a conference talk reported in blogs, not a paper. — [Battelle, "Marissa Mayer on Googley Lessons"](https://battellemedia.com/archives/2006/11/marissa_mayer_on_googley_lessons)
- [data displays, guideline] Smith & Mosier 2.5/9 "Adequate Window Size": "When a display window must be used for data scanning, ensure that the window can display more than one line of data." — [Smith & Mosier](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~cs3283/ftp/guidelines.pdf)

### Inferences
- Kelly & Azzopardi's three-per-page group paged on until they had seen about ten items. In a short dashboard widget, a page size that follows height (say 3 rows) could produce exactly that pattern: many page turns for the same coverage. The study is about search results, so this is an analogy, not a finding about tables.
- Since users seldom change settings (Mackay [S]) and Baymard expects the selector to be underused, the [10, 25, 50] selector cannot be the main answer to "the table should fill its frame". Whatever the table does with no configuration decides the experience.
- Nielsen's ceiling of about 100 items for View All, and Baymard's 50–100 before "Load more", are the only published numbers for how much one scrollable view should hold. Both come from web, not from tables.

### Gaps
- No usage figures for page-size selectors in data tables or business applications were found. Baymard's guideline text with figures is behind Premium.
- No study varies page size by available height.

## 3. Layout stability: CLS, spatial memory, rows moving pages, pager under the pointer

### Takeaway
Three bodies of evidence converge on predictability. (a) The web's layout-shift metric treats shifts as acceptable only when the user expects them. It does not count shifts within 500 ms of a discrete input, and it treats viewport resizes as such input. (b) Spatial-memory research finds that layouts which keep items in place beat layouts that reflow, and the gap is dramatic when a window is scaled. (c) Adaptive-UI studies find static layouts faster than system-adapted ones, and predictability raises satisfaction. They also find that accurate adaptation can still pay off, which tempers a blanket "never adapt". NUREG-0700 adds that automated interface changes must be anticipatable, observable and not distracting. A pager that moves under a repeatedly clicking pointer is the textbook unexpected shift. A row that moves to another page because a pane was resized breaks spatial memory and "page 3" re-finding, even though CLS would not count it.

### Cited Findings
- [web metric] web.dev, CLS: "A layout shift is only bad if the user isn't expecting it." "Layout shifts that occur within 500 milliseconds of user input will have the hadRecentInput flag set, so they can be excluded from calculations." This applies to discrete events only: "Continuous interactions such as scrolls, drags, or pinch and zoom gestures are not considered "recent input"." On slow responses: "create some space right away and show a loading indicator", otherwise the user may click "something that could move out from under them". — [web.dev/articles/cls](https://web.dev/articles/cls)
- [web metric] web.dev, CLS: "content that moves gradually and naturally from one position to the next can often help the user better understand what's going on". — [web.dev/articles/cls](https://web.dev/articles/cls)
- [web spec] Layout Instability API §2.4: "An excluding input is any event from an input device which signals a user’s active interaction with the document, or any event which directly changes the size of the viewport." "The mousemove and pointermove events are also not excluding inputs." — [WICG Layout Instability](https://wicg.github.io/layout-instability/)
- [web guidance] web.dev, Optimize CLS: "Try to avoid collapsing the reserved space by showing a placeholder". "Removing the space set aside for elements can cause just as much CLS as inserting content." For user-triggered updates, offer a "Load more" or "Refresh" button so users "are not surprised by the shift". — [web.dev/articles/optimize-cls](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-cls)
- [spatial memory, peer-reviewed, abstract only] Scarr, Cockburn, Gutwin & Malacria, CHI 2013: "Spatial consistency allows users to develop memory of object locations, reducing the time needed for visual search". Changes such as a window "moved to a new location, scaled, or rotated" did not strongly hurt performance, except large rotations. "This study showed that spatial stability with scaling dramatically outperforms reflowing." — [University of Calgary repository](https://ucalgary.scholaris.ca/items/002efab2-3e14-4ee7-b7e3-6a3f79fae67f/full), [doi](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2466430)
- [menus, peer-reviewed, full text] Findlater & McGrenere, CHI 2004, 27 subjects: "The static menu was found to be significantly faster than the adaptive menu." 15 of 27 preferred the user-controlled (adaptable) menu. "six people commented on the inconsistency of the adaptive menus, saying that this made the menus frustrating to use." — [CHI 2004 PDF](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/labs/edapt/papers/findlater2004.pdf)
- [toolbars, peer-reviewed, full text] Gajos et al., CHI 2008, 23 participants: "critics argue that adaptation's unpredictability may disorient users". "increasing predictability and accuracy led to strongly improved satisfaction", but "improvement in accuracy had a stronger effect on performance, utilization and some satisfaction ratings than the improvement in predictability". The paper defines an algorithm as predictable "if it follows a strategy users can easily model in their heads". — [CHI 2008 author PDF](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/kgajos-chi08-predictability.pdf)
- [control rooms, guideline] NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, 2.5.7 "Automated Actions". 2.5.7-1: "Automated interface management features should be designed such that their operation can be anticipated by users." 2.5.7-2: "The operation of automated interface management features should be apparent to the user." 2.5.7-3: it "should not draw excessive attention from the user." — [NUREG-0700 Rev. 3](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)
- [data displays, guideline] Smith & Mosier 2.5/1: "Consistent display formats will help establish and preserve user orientation." 2.0/6: "When an item is missing from a standard format, display that item as a labeled blank rather than omitting it altogether." — [Smith & Mosier](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~cs3283/ftp/guidelines.pdf)
- [data displays, guideline] Smith & Mosier 2.7.2/3 and NUREG 1.2.2-13: number items in multipage lists "continuously in relation to the first item on the first page". NUREG 1.2.2-17: "Where lists extend over more than one display page, the last line of one page should be the first line on the succeeding page." — [Smith & Mosier](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~cs3283/ftp/guidelines.pdf), [NUREG-0700 Rev. 3](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)
- [web, general] NN/g on change blindness (Budiu, 2018): people miss changes "in a region that is far away from their focus of attention", and when "movement as a cue for change is weak or completely absent". — [Change Blindness in UX](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/change-blindness-definition/)

### Inferences
- **Pager under the pointer.** A click on Next is a discrete input, so if the pager then moves, CLS does not count the shift. The metric's exclusion assumes that a shift following a click is expected. A user clicking Next five times expects the button to stay put, and web.dev names that exact failure ("move out from under them"). So the current filler, which keeps the pager in place, is backed by the guidance even though the metric would not penalise its absence.
- **Resize changes the page size (option 1).** By the Layout Instability spec, a browser-window resize is an excluding input, so the reflow is "expected" as far as the metric goes. A pane splitter or a dashboard-widget resize is a pointer drag: only the pointerdown is excluding, so shifts more than 500 ms into a long drag would count. A size change caused by something else (a neighbour panel opening, a banner) is not user input at all. The metric therefore treats some resize sources as expected and others as unexpected; the user sees no such difference.
- Scarr et al. compared a stable, scaled layout with a reflowed one. Re-chunking rows into pages on resize is a reflow of the record space: record 41 moves from page 3 to page 2. The finding points against option 1 unless the anchor record stays in view.
- Gajos et al. temper this: adaptation that is accurate and has a rule users can model ("as many rows as fit") need not hurt. NUREG 2.5.7 gives the conditions: the user must be able to anticipate it (they resized it themselves), see it (the row range in the pager changes), and not be distracted by it (no animation storm).
- **Continuous numbering.** NUREG 1.2.2-13 and Smith & Mosier 2.7.2/3 suggest a position cue that survives a page-size change: "Rows 41–60 of 230", or row numbers, rather than "Page 3 of 12" alone. NUREG 1.2.2-17's one-line overlap between pages is the opposite of web practice and gives continuity; I found no web-UX test of it.

### Gaps
- I did not read the full text of Scarr et al. 2013, or their monograph *Supporting and Exploiting Spatial Memory in User Interfaces* ([doi](https://doi.org/10.1561/1100000046), 403 to the fetch tool). So the size of the effect and the exact reflow condition are unknown.
- No study tests a paged list whose page boundaries move under the user.

## 4. Blank space under content, filler rows, empty ruled rows

### Takeaway
I found no direct research on blank filler under the rows of a table, or on empty ruled rows. The adjacent evidence says three things. (a) A totally empty area causes doubt about whether the system works, so emptiness needs words. (b) Large whitespace signals "the end", which is the correct message after a short last page. (c) Blank space structures dense displays, and the human-factors guidelines even ask for a blank row every four or five rows. Placeholder shapes that mimic content read as loading (skeleton screens), and visible boundaries act as signifiers of interactivity. So empty ruled rows are likely to read as loading or as editable slots. That is an inference, not a tested result.

### Cited Findings
- [complex apps] NN/g (Kaplan, 2021): "Totally empty states cause confusion about how and whether the system is working." Her example is a blank log table after a date-range query; she recommends text such as "There are no records to display for the selected date range". — [Designing Empty States in Complex Applications](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/)
- [web, general] NN/g (Flaherty, 2016): "the large gap between content elements can signal the end of a page". A full-width line "can create a visual barrier and discourage people to scroll further". — [The Illusion of Completeness](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/illusion-of-completeness/)
- [loading] NN/g (Tankala, 2023): content-placeholder skeletons are boxes whose "structure … mimics the structure of the final page". A frame-display skeleton "is not recommended" because users may think the page is broken. — [Skeleton Screens 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeleton-screens/)
- [web, eyetracking] NN/g (Moran, 2017; 71 users, 9 pages × 2 versions): "participants spent 22% more time (i.e., slower task performance) looking at the pages with weak signifiers". — [Flat UI Elements Attract Less Attention and Cause Uncertainty](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/)
- [data displays, guideline] Smith & Mosier 2.5/3 "Spacing to Structure Displays": "Use blank space to structure a display … Closely packed data are difficult to locate and difficult to read." 2.7.2/5: mark continued lists "continued on next page" or "more", and concluded lists "end of list". "Exception: Short lists whose conclusion is evident from the display format need not be annotated in this way." — [Smith & Mosier](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~cs3283/ftp/guidelines.pdf)
- [control rooms, guideline] NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, 1.2.2-7: "In dense tables with many rows, a blank line, dots, or some other distinctive feature … should be inserted after a group of rows at regular intervals … a blank line after every five rows." 4.2.8-3: "Alphanumeric alarm lists should have a separation (blank row) between every four or five alphanumeric messages." 1.5-8: "Display packing density should not exceed 50 percent … The unused area should be distributed to separate logical groups, rather than having all unused areas on one side." — [NUREG-0700 Rev. 3](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)

### Inferences
- **Blank filler after a short last page or a search.** The table has rows, then the space below them is empty. That is not a "totally empty state". The illusion-of-completeness finding says the space reads as "end". On the last page that is true, so the filler tells the truth. It reads wrong only where more data exists below it, for example a scroll container that looks finished.
- **Filler on a page that is not the last.** Under option 2 or 3, a pageSize-10 table in a 600 px pane shows 10 rows and then a blank band, with more pages still to come. Here the blank says "end" while the pager says "page 1 of 5", so the two cues contradict each other. This is the owner's objection, and the illusion-of-completeness evidence supports it.
- **Empty ruled rows (option 3).** Ruled empty lines copy the structure of real rows, which is what a content-placeholder skeleton does, so users may read them as loading. In spreadsheets, empty ruled cells are where you type, so users may also read them as slots to fill. NN/g's signifier work shows that visible structure is read as a cue to act. Neither reading is "nothing more here". NUREG's blank row every 4–5 rows is a separator inside data, not a ruled empty row, so it does not support option 3.
- NUREG 1.5-8's "rather than having all unused areas on one side" is about display layout in general. Read loosely, it argues against one big blank block at the bottom of a frame. That is a weak, analogical point.

### Gaps
- No primary research was found on the perception of blank filler in tables, or on empty ruled rows. Design-system pages and mailing-list threads disagree, and they are not primary research, so I did not use them.

## 5. Empty and loading states

### Takeaway
An empty table must say why it is empty: no data yet, no results for this filter or search, or still loading. Showing "no records" while still loading is particularly harmful. Timing rules are stable across NN/g sources. Under about 1 s, show no indicator, because a flash distracts. From 1 s to about 10 s, show a looped indicator, or a skeleton for full-view loads. Over 10 s, show percent-done and a way to cancel. Spinners suit single modules such as a dashboard card. The human-factors guidelines add that a display must not blank out during an update.

### Cited Findings
- [complex apps] NN/g (Kaplan, 2021): use empty states "to communicate system status". The article covers "Search results lists when nothing is found" as well as first use. "Inaccurate system-status messages for empty states are particularly harmful." — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/empty-state-interface-design/)
- [classic] Nielsen 1993/2014: "0.1 second is about the limit for having the user feel that the system is reacting instantaneously"; "1.0 second is about the limit for the user's flow of thought to stay uninterrupted"; "10 seconds is about the limit for keeping the user's attention focused on the dialogue". — [Response Times: The 3 Important Limits](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/)
- [general] NN/g (Sherwin, 2014): "Use a progress indicator for any action that takes longer than about 1.0 second". "For anything that takes less than 1 second to load, it is distracting to use a looped animation". Use a looped indicator for 2–10 s and percent-done for 10 s or more. — [Progress Indicators](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/progress-indicators/)
- [general] NN/g (Tankala, 2023): "If a page takes less than 1 second to load, skeleton screens or spinners aren't necessary". Spinners are "typically best used on a single module, like a video or a card which is on a dashboard". Skeletons "are better when the full screen is loading". — [Skeleton Screens 101](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/skeleton-screens/)
- [data displays, guideline] Smith & Mosier 2.0/1: "even temporary loss of needed data, as might be caused by display blanking during automatic data update, is not acceptable in many design applications". It also asks that "header information should be retained or generated anew when a user is paging/scrolling data tables". — [Smith & Mosier](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~cs3283/ftp/guidelines.pdf)
- [web guidance] web.dev: for interactions that trigger a slow request, "create some space right away and show a loading indicator". — [web.dev/articles/cls](https://web.dev/articles/cls)

### Inferences
- A table that fills its frame has a natural home for the empty message: the middle of the empty body, with the column headers kept (Smith & Mosier 2.0/1, 2.0/6). Different wording for "No entries yet" and "No entries match 'xyz'" follows NN/g's distinction.
- On a page change backed by a server, keep the old rows visible and dimmed, with `aria-busy` set, until the new ones arrive. Do not blank them or swap in skeleton rows for the first second. This follows both the 1-second rule and the no-blanking rule.

### Gaps
- None of these sources gives figures for tables specifically. The NN/g timing rules come from general UI research.

## 6. Accessibility: WCAG 2.2 and WAI-ARIA

### Takeaway
Page and count changes ("Rows 11–20 of 120", "No entries match") are status messages under SC 4.1.3. Announce them through `role="status"` without moving focus; the results list itself is not a status message. WAI-ARIA says `aria-rowcount` and `aria-rowindex` are needed whenever "only a portion of the rows is present in the DOM". Read literally, that covers a paged table. The APG data-grid example uses them on a grid with 15 data rows, not all of them shown, and sets `aria-rowcount="16"`, so the header row is evidently counted. In a grid, Page Down and Page Up move "an author-determined number of rows". Focus must never be lost: keep it on the control that was used, or move it to a predictable neighbour. Motion caused by an interaction must be possible to switch off (2.3.3, AAA). Tables are exempt from Reflow, but their pager is not. Fixed row heights must not clip text under user text spacing (1.4.12).

### Cited Findings
- WCAG 2.2, Understanding 4.1.3 Status Messages: status messages must be "programmatically determined … such that they can be presented to the user by assistive technologies without receiving focus". "18 results returned" and "No results returned" are status messages; "the list of results obtained from a search" is not. — [Understanding SC 4.1.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/status-messages.html)
- WAI-ARIA 1.2, `aria-rowcount`: "If all of the rows are present in the DOM, it is not necessary to set this attribute … However, if only a portion of the rows is present in the DOM at a given moment, this attribute is needed to provide an explicit indication of the number of rows in the full table." Use -1 if the total is unknown. `aria-rowindex` uses the same wording, and "Authors SHOULD place aria-rowindex on each row." — [WAI-ARIA 1.2 #aria-rowcount](https://www.w3.org/TR/wai-aria-1.2/#aria-rowcount), [#aria-rowindex](https://www.w3.org/TR/wai-aria-1.2/#aria-rowindex)
- WAI-ARIA 1.2, `aria-busy`: "When aria-busy is true for an element, assistive technologies MAY ignore changes to content owned by that element and then process all changes made during the busy period as a single, atomic update". — [WAI-ARIA 1.2 #aria-busy](https://www.w3.org/TR/wai-aria-1.2/#aria-busy)
- APG Grid pattern: Page Down "Moves focus down an author-determined number of rows"; Control + Home goes to "the first cell in the first row". With rows missing from the DOM, Control + End "may move focus to the last row in the DOM rather than the last available row in the back-end data". "The grid pattern is particularly useful if the tabular information is editable or interactive." — [APG Grid Pattern](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/grid/)
- APG Data Grid Example 3 has 15 data rows and `aria-rowcount="16"`. Page Down "moves focus down five rows, scrolling so the bottom row in the currently visible set of rows becomes the first visible row". Example 1 leaves out Page Down and Page Up because "such scroll-like functions are not useful with so few rows". — [APG Data Grid Examples](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/patterns/grid/examples/data-grids/)
- APG, Developing a Keyboard Interface: "It is essential that there is always a component within the user interface that is active." When an item is deleted, set focus "on the list item following the deleted item". "Usability of a keyboard interface is heavily influenced by how readily users can guess where focus will land." — [APG Keyboard Interface](https://www.w3.org/WAI/ARIA/apg/practices/keyboard-interface/)
- WCAG 2.2, 2.4.3 Focus Order: focusable components "receive focus in an order that preserves meaning and operability". — [Understanding SC 2.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-order.html)
- WCAG 2.2, 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum, AA): "When a user interface component receives keyboard focus, the component is not entirely hidden due to author-created content". The Understanding page names sticky headers and footers as the typical cause, with scroll padding (C43) as a fix and F110 as the failure. — [Understanding SC 2.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/focus-not-obscured-minimum.html)
- WCAG 2.2, 1.3.2 Meaningful Sequence: "Tables and ordered lists are meaningful sequences". C27 asks that DOM order match visual order. — [Understanding SC 1.3.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/meaningful-sequence.html)
- WCAG 2.2, 2.3.3 Animation from Interactions (AAA): "Motion animation triggered by interaction can be disabled, unless the animation is essential". Technique C39 uses `prefers-reduced-motion`. — [Understanding SC 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)
- WCAG 2.2, 1.4.10 Reflow (AA): data tables are excepted, but controls around them, pagination included, must still reflow. "256 CSS pixels is equivalent to a starting viewport height of 1024 CSS pixels at 400% zoom." — [Understanding SC 1.4.10](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/reflow.html)
- WCAG 2.2, 1.4.12 Text Spacing (AA): line height 1.5 × font size and so on, "no loss of content or functionality". Clipped text with no way to show it fails (F104). — [Understanding SC 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)
- WCAG 2.2, 2.2.2 Pause, Stop, Hide: "For any auto-updating information that (1) starts automatically and (2) is presented in parallel with other content, there is a mechanism for the user to pause, stop, or hide it or to control the frequency of the update". Real-time status may jump to current on resume. — [Understanding SC 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)
- WCAG 2.2, 3.2.2 On Input: "A change of content is not always a change of context". — [Understanding SC 3.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/on-input.html)

### Inferences
- **Paged tables and `aria-rowcount`.** The spec's condition is met on every page of a paged table, so set `aria-rowcount` to the filtered total plus header rows, and `aria-rowindex` on each row. A screen reader can then say "row 42 of 231" on page 3. The APG never discusses pagination, so this is a reading of the spec, not stated guidance.
- **Page change.** Focus stays on the Next or Back button that was activated. One live `role="status"` announces "Rows 21–30 of 120" (4.1.3). If Next becomes disabled on the last page, focus is stranded on a disabled control. Either keep the control focusable, using `aria-disabled` rather than `disabled`, or move focus predictably to Back (APG "always a component … active"). If focus was in a row (grid mode), move it to the same row index on the new page, or to the first row.
- **Page-size change** (selector, or height under option 1). Keep focus on the focused record if it is still on the page. If not, move to the page that contains it rather than resetting to page 1, so focus has somewhere predictable to land. Announce the new range. Do not announce anything during a continuous resize; announce once when it settles.
- **Option 1 and zoom.** Under option 1, zooming to 200–400 % or applying 1.4.12 text spacing makes rows taller and fewer. At the 256 px reflow height, perhaps 4–6 rows fit. Page size then depends on the user's accessibility settings, which is acceptable as long as the range text and `aria-rowindex` carry the position. A fixed row pitch with `overflow: hidden` risks failing 1.4.12, under any option.
- **Page Down in grid mode.** With a page size that follows height, "author-determined" can be the number of visible rows. That matches the desktop convention and APG Example 3's "bottom row … becomes the first visible row".

### Gaps
- I found no published test of screen readers announcing `aria-rowindex` in a paginated HTML table.
- WCAG has no criterion that names focus loss when the focused element is removed. The APG keyboard practice is guidance, not a conformance requirement.

## 7. Motion: do animated transitions help users follow added or removed rows?

### Takeaway
Controlled studies show that well-designed animated transitions help people track change: object constancy (Heer & Robertson, 24 subjects) and mental maps (Bederson & Boltman, 20 subjects). Both studies used about 1 s transitions. Heer & Robertson used a fade for items entering and leaving, not motion, to avoid "nonmeaningful changes". The counter-evidence: equivalence-controlled reviews find animation rarely beats static displays (Tversky et al.); staggering can hurt tracking (Chevalier et al., [S]); and UI animation should be 100–400 ms, shorter the more often it is seen, and removable under reduced motion. Paging itself should be discrete (NUREG 2.5.6-14).

### Cited Findings
- [data graphics, peer-reviewed, full text] Heer & Robertson, InfoVis 2007, 24 subjects: "animated transitions can significantly improve graphical perception". Their principles include "Use simple transitions" and "Make transitions as long as needed, but no longer", with transitions of "around 1 second". For filtered items, "fading items in and out using alpha blending. This also avoids the nonmeaningful changes inherent in these other movements." — [PDF](https://idl.cs.washington.edu/files/2007-AnimatedTransitions-InfoVis.pdf), [paper page](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/animated-transitions/)
- [spatial navigation, peer-reviewed, full text of TR] Bederson & Boltman, InfoVis 1999, 20 subjects: "animation improves users' ability to reconstruct the information space, with no penalty on task performance time"; "animations of one second seem to strike a balance". — [CS-TR-3964 PDF](https://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/hcil/jazz/learn/papers/CS-TR-3964.pdf)
- [review, peer-reviewed, abstract read] Tversky, Morrison & Bétrancourt, IJHCS 2002: "the research on the efficacy of animated over static graphics is not encouraging … Animations are often too complex or too fast to be accurately perceived". The authors still allow benefits "for real time reorientations in time and space". — [PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/aragon/classes/hcde411/w13/readings/Tversky_AnimationFacilitate_IJHCS02.pdf)
- [visual tracking, peer-reviewed, S] Chevalier, Dragicevic & Franconeri, TVCG 2014: staggering had little effect on tracking and sometimes hurt it, through lost common-motion grouping and less predictable start times. — [HAL record](https://hal.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01054408v1)
- [UI practice] NN/g (Laubheimer, 2020): "the duration of most animations should be in the range of 100–500 ms"; "At 500ms, animations start to feel like a real drag for users". Frequently seen animations should be shorter and subtler. Respect a user's "reduce motion" setting. — [Executing UX Animations](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)
- [UI practice] NN/g (Budiu, 2018): "Use animation to signal change", but avoid many competing animations. — [Change Blindness](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/change-blindness-definition/)
- [web guidance] web.dev: "Composited animations using `translate` can't impact other elements, and so don't count toward CLS." — [Optimize CLS](https://web.dev/articles/optimize-cls)
- [control rooms, guideline] NUREG-0700 2.5.6-14 "Discrete Paging": "no display of intermediate pages between the starting page and the selected page". — [NUREG-0700 Rev. 3](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)
- [standard] WCAG 2.3.3 (AAA): interaction-triggered motion must be possible to switch off; C39 `prefers-reduced-motion`. — [Understanding SC 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html)

### Inferences
- Under option 1, a resize adds or removes rows at the bottom of the page while the rows above stay where they are. Nothing needs tracking except the bottom edge. A short fade (Heer & Robertson's entry/exit treatment) is enough. Sliding motion would add "nonmeaningful changes". During a continuous drag, no animation at all is better; the rows simply appear or disappear.
- The 1 s transitions in the research were one-off, analytical transitions. NN/g's 100–400 ms, "shorter the more often seen", fits a control that is used constantly. A page turn should be an instant swap (NUREG 2.5.6-14), not a slide.

### Gaps
- No study of animated row insertion or removal in tables was found. The Chevalier et al. full text was not obtained.

## 8. Dashboards, glanceable displays and industrial alarm lists

### Takeaway
Dashboards are meant to be "single-screen", at-a-glance views (NN/g), but I found no primary guidance on tables inside dashboard widgets. The strongest evidence is from the nuclear regulator's review guideline NUREG-0700, which is public. All high-priority alarms must be visible at once ("Operators should never have to page or scroll a display to view high-priority alarms"). Incoming alarms should not scroll the list; "paging, is preferred". Overflow belongs on later pages, clearly indicated. A newest-first chronological option should be offered. EEMUA 191 Ed. 4 has sections titled "Positioning of new alarms on the list" and "Movement through the list", but they are paywalled and were not read. ISA-18.2 and ISA-101 were not read.

### Cited Findings
- [dashboards] NN/g (Laubheimer, 2017): dashboards are "collections of data visualizations, presented in a single-page view". "Operational dashboards aim to impart critical information quickly to users as they are engaged in time-sensitive tasks". Analytical dashboards also need "an at-a-glance, single-screen view". The article does not mention tables, scrolling or item limits. — [Dashboards: Making Charts and Graphs Easier to Understand](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dashboards-preattentive/)
- [web, attention] NN/g (Fessenden, 2018; 120 participants, 130,000+ fixations): "users spent about 57% of their page-viewing time above the fold"; people "rarely go beyond the third screenful of info". — [Scrolling and Attention](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- [control rooms, guideline] NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, 4.2.2-2: for message lists, "a sufficient display area should be provided for the simultaneous viewing of all high-priority alarms … under the worst credible conditions. Operators should never have to page or scroll a display to view high-priority alarms." — [NUREG-0700 Rev. 3](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)
- NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, 4.2.8-4 "Scrolling of Message List": "The method of adding alarm messages to the list should preclude message scrolling … Scrolling makes it difficult to read alarm messages, especially when many alarms are coming in. An alternative method of viewing alarm lists, such as paging, is preferred." 4.2.8-5: overflow "should be kept on subsequent alarm pages … the alarm system should clearly indicate that additional information is available in subsequent pages." — [same](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)
- NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, 4.2.8-1: lists are "segregated by alarm priority with the highest priority alarms being listed first". 4.2.8-2: users should be able to list alarms "in chronological order with the most recent messages placed at the top of the stack". — [same](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)
- NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, 4.2.9-1: when a display cannot show all alarms, show "only the most important (highest priority)", or "a high-level indication that a relevant alarm exists". — [same](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)
- NUREG-0700 Rev. 3, 1.5-10: "Each page of a multipage display sequence should be numbered. Typically, the phrase “page x of y” is used". 1.5-15: in scrollable tables, "all labeling information should be preserved". — [same](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/1644018)
- [data displays, guideline] Smith & Mosier 2.7.3/3: for changing values, "data should be displayed in a fixed position and updated no more than once per second". 2.7.3/5 "Display Freeze": let users stop automatic updates to examine changed data. — [Smith & Mosier](https://www.comp.nus.edu.sg/~cs3283/ftp/guidelines.pdf)
- [process industry, title only] EEMUA 191 Edition 4 (Nov 2024), contents: 5.2.2 "Alarm list displays", 5.5 "Design of alarm lists", 5.5.3 "Positioning of new alarms on the list", 5.5.5 "Movement through the list", 5.8 "The full alarm list", and Figure 17 "Alarm state transition diagram for static alarm list display". The text is paywalled and was not read. — [EEMUA contents PDF](https://eemua.org/getattachment/9d3f8071-55c3-49bf-a74a-3bf6ad4a2e0f/Contents-EEMUA-Publication-191-Edition4-November-2024.pdf)
- [process industry, peer-reviewed, S] An ASM Consortium study with 24 operators compared a list-based alarm summary with a time-series "alarm tracker". The response strategy mattered more than the format. — [doi 10.1016/j.ergon.2013.11.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ergon.2013.11.008)
- [nuclear, S] NUREG/CR-6691 (six two-person crews; tile, message-list and integrated alarm formats): modest performance differences, with ratings more sensitive to design. — [NRC NUREG/CR-6691](https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/nuregs/contract/cr6691/)
- [vendor, S] Rockwell FactoryTalk View's alarm list puts the newest alarms at the top and stops auto-scrolling once the operator moves the highlight away from the top. — [Rockwell help](https://www.rockwellautomation.com/en-us/docs/factorytalk-view/16-00-00/me-help-ditamap/graphic-objects-drawing-elements/create-an-alarm-list/alarm-list.html) (403 to the fetch tool)

### Inferences
- For alarm lists, NUREG implies **fill and page**: use all the space for simultaneous viewing (4.2.2-2), page rather than scroll (4.2.8-4), and signal overflow (4.2.8-5). The page size that best satisfies "sufficient display area" is as many rows as fit, which is option 1, in the one domain whose standards speak to it. NUREG 2.5.7's anticipation rule still applies: the operator resizing a pane is anticipated, but a list re-chunking while alarms arrive is not.
- A newest-first list on page 1 shifts rows down as alarms arrive. That conflicts with "fixed position" (Smith & Mosier 2.7.3/3) and is why the vendor list freezes when the operator moves away from the top. This concerns row stability within a page, not page size.
- For dashboard widgets, NN/g's single-screen ideal plus NUREG 4.2.9-1 (show the most important when space is limited) point to "the top N rows that fit, sorted by importance, plus a link or count to the rest", rather than a full pager inside a small card. The evidence is thin: no source addresses tables in widgets directly.

### Gaps
- The texts of ISA-18.2, ISA-101, IEC 62682 and EEMUA 191 were not read. NUREG-0700 Revision 4 has been issued; the NRC says it has minor updates to Sections 1, 2, 6, 7 and 10 [S], so the Rev. 3 wording of the section 1 and 2 guidelines quoted here may have changed.
- No primary guidance on the number of rows in a dashboard table widget was found.

## Summary for the design question

**Option 1: page size follows the available height.**
- For: it fills the frame with real data, which serves comparison across rows (NN/g table tasks) and seeing relationships (Smith & Mosier 2.7.2/1–2). It is the reading of NUREG-0700 that fits alarm lists best: simultaneous viewing in the available area, paging over scrolling, overflow signalled. Since users seldom change page size (Baymard "likely underused"; Mackay [S]), only a default that adapts serves most users. Adaptation with a simple, modelable rule ("as many as fit") need not hurt: Gajos et al. find that accuracy outweighs predictability. A window resize counts as expected input for the layout-shift metric.
- Against: it breaks re-finding by page ("page 3", NN/g) and moves records between pages, the reflow that Scarr et al. find "dramatically" worse than spatial stability. Pane, splitter and widget resizes and other sources of size change are not "expected" in the metric's terms. NUREG 2.5.7 requires automated changes to be anticipatable, observable and not distracting. In small frames it yields tiny pages (3–5 rows), and Kelly & Azzopardi show that small pages change behaviour (more paging, attention on the first items). Page size also comes to depend on zoom and text spacing.

**Option 2: paginated tables never fill; only scrolling tables fill.**
- For: page boundaries never move, so "page 3" stays true (NN/g), and the layout is static, which Findlater & McGrenere found faster than adaptive. It is simple to model.
- Against: in a tall frame it leaves the owner's "big blank surface" under 10 rows while the pager says there are more pages. The illusion-of-completeness finding (whitespace reads as "end") makes that blank band contradict the pager. It does not meet NUREG's sufficient-display-area rule for alarm lists. The default [10, 25, 50] cannot rescue it, since selectors are underused.

**Option 3: fill with empty ruled rows.**
- For: the frame looks finished and the pager stays put.
- Against: I found no primary research either way. By analogy, ruled placeholders mimic content structure the way skeletons do (NN/g: read as loading), and visible structure is read as a signifier of interactivity or an editable slot, as in spreadsheets. NN/g's empty-state finding says emptiness must be explained in words, not decorated. NUREG's blank rows are separators inside data, not padding.

**What the evidence says a delightful solution must do:**
1. **Keep the pager in place** under repeated clicks: reserve the space and never collapse it (web.dev). Keep column headers visible on every page and while scrolling (Smith & Mosier 2.7.2/4; NUREG 1.5-15).
2. **Fill the frame with real rows when it can, and say so when it cannot.** Blank space after the last row is honest only when there really is nothing more. On a page that is not the last, blank space reads as "end" and contradicts the pager.
3. **Make position survive any change of page size.** Show a row range and total ("Rows 41–60 of 230"), not only "Page 3 of 12" (Smith & Mosier 2.7.2/3, NUREG 1.2.2-13). Keep an anchor record (the focused or selected row, or else the first visible row) in view when the page size changes. Expose the position to assistive technology with `aria-rowcount` and `aria-rowindex`.
4. **Only adapt at moments the user can anticipate** (NUREG 2.5.7). Apply a height-driven page size when the resize settles, not row by row during a drag, and never while alarms arrive into a newest-first list. Remember an explicit page-size choice (Nielsen) and let it override the adaptation.
5. **Explain emptiness and waiting.** Use distinct words for "no data" and "no results for this filter", shown inside the frame with headers kept (NN/g). Show nothing for responses under 1 s, a local indicator from 1 to 10 s, and never blank the old rows during an update (Smith & Mosier 2.0/1). Use `aria-busy` during the swap.
6. **Announce, don't steal focus.** One `role="status"` region carries the range and result-count changes (4.1.3). Focus stays on the control that was used, and never lands on a disabled control. In grid mode, Page Down moves by the visible row count (APG).
7. **Keep motion out of paging.** Page turns are instant and discrete (NUREG 2.5.6-14). Rows added or removed by a resize may fade in 100–200 ms (Heer & Robertson's fade, NN/g's durations), with no motion under `prefers-reduced-motion` (WCAG 2.3.3).
8. **In alarm lists, show all high-priority alarms without paging or scrolling** (NUREG 4.2.2-2). Let new alarms page rather than scroll (4.2.8-4), signal overflow pages (4.2.8-5), and freeze automatic movement while the operator is working in the list (Smith & Mosier 2.7.3/5).
